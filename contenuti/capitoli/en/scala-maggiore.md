Almost every piece you will sing uses a specific group of notes, and one of them acts as the center. This group of notes, put in order, is a scale. Knowing how to build it means understanding why a piece has certain sharps or flats, and not others.

## The scale and its degrees

A scale starts from one note, the **tonic**, and goes up one note at a time to the tonic an octave higher. Each note name appears only once: after C comes a D, never a C♯. The notes of the scale are called degrees and are numbered from the tonic: first degree, second degree, up to the seventh, and the eighth is the tonic again.

Some degrees also have a name. The first is the tonic, the fifth the dominant, the seventh the leading tone (UK: leading note), because it sits just below the tonic and the melody tends to rise toward it.

```abc
X:1
L:1/4
K:C
C D E F | G A B c |]
```

This is the C major scale, from C4 to C5: all white keys, no accidentals.

## Whole steps and half steps in a row

A major scale is not just any row of notes: the distances between one degree and the next always follow the same order. Calling the whole step W and the half step H, the order is W W H W W W H.

In the C major scale you can see it on the keyboard. C and D are a whole step apart, D and E a whole step, E and F a half step, then three whole steps (F G, G A, A B) and finally a half step between B and C. The two half steps fall between the third and fourth degrees and between the seventh and eighth.

## Major scales on other notes

If the scale starts from another note, the order W W H W W W H stays the same, and keeping it takes accidentals. Try starting from D: D and E are a whole step apart, which is right. Between E and the next degree you need a whole step, but E and F are a half step apart; so the third degree becomes F♯, and F♯ and G are the half step you need. In the same way, at the end, the seventh degree becomes C♯.

```abc
X:2
L:1/4
K:C
D E ^F G | A B ^c d |]
```

So D major has two sharps, F♯ and C♯. Starting from F, on the other hand, between the third and fourth degrees (A and B) you need a half step, and B becomes B♭. It is written B♭ and not A♯, even though on the keyboard it is the same key, because in a scale each name appears once and A is already there.

## Key and key signature

A piece built on the D major scale is **in D major**: the key is the pair of the tonic (D) and the mode (major). Writing F♯ and C♯ in front of every F and every C would be awkward. So they are written just once, at the start of every staff, right after the clef: this is the key signature.

```abc
X:3
L:1/4
K:D
D E F G | A B c d |]
```

The notes are the same as in the previous example, but the two sharps are in the key signature. A sharp in the key signature applies to every note with that name, in every octave and for the whole piece, until the key signature changes.

## The order of sharps and flats

Sharps always appear in the key signature in the same order: F, C, G, D, A, E, B. A key with two sharps has F♯ and C♯, one with three adds G♯, and so on. Flats come in the opposite order: B, E, A, D, G, C, F.

```abc
X:4
L:1/1
K:C#
x |]
```

```abc
X:5
L:1/1
K:Cb
x |]
```

Above are the two complete key signatures, with seven sharps and with seven flats.

Two rules let you find the major key by looking at the key signature:

- with sharps, the tonic is a half step above the last sharp: with three sharps the last one is G♯, and the tonic is A;
- with flats, the tonic is the second-to-last flat: with three flats the second-to-last is E♭, and the tonic is E♭.

Two cases are left out and are worth learning by heart: with no accidentals it is C major, with a single flat it is F major.

## The circle of fifths

Each extra sharp moves the tonic five notes higher, counting the starting note too: C, G, D, A, E, B, F♯, C♯. Each extra flat moves it five notes lower: C, F, B♭, E♭, A♭, D♭, G♭, C♭. This distance is called a fifth, and you will meet it again in chapter 8.

Arranged in a circle, with C at the top, the sharps on one side and the flats on the other, the keys form the circle of fifths. At the bottom the two sides meet: F♯ major, with six sharps, and G♭ major, with six flats, sound the same and are two names for the same key.

The circle is mostly useful for seeing which keys are close to each other. Two neighboring keys have key signatures that differ by a single accidental, and a piece moves more easily from one to the other.
