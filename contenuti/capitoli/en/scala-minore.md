Many choral pieces are in minor. The minor scale uses the same notes as a major scale, but starts from a different point, and that is why it sounds different. It also has three forms, and you will meet all of them in the music you sing.

## The relative minor

Take the notes of C major and start from the sixth degree, A: A B C D E F G A. They are the same white keys, but the tonic has changed, and with it the order of whole and half steps: W H W W H W W. This is the A minor scale.

```abc
X:1
L:1/4
K:Am
A B c d | e f g a |]
```

C major and A minor have the same notes and the same key signature, with no accidentals. A minor is called the **relative minor** of C major, and C major the relative major of A minor.

Every major key has its relative minor, and its tonic is always a minor third below, that is three half steps lower. Counting note names, you go down two: from C to A, from G to E, from F to D, from D to B.

So to find the minor key from a key signature, you first find the major key with the rules from chapter 5 and then go down three half steps. With one sharp the major key is G, and the minor key is E; with two flats the major key is B♭, and the minor key is G.

## Natural minor

The scale you have just seen, using only the notes of the key signature, is called natural minor. Compared with major, its third, sixth, and seventh degrees are a half step lower, measuring from the tonic.

```abc
X:2
L:1/4
K:Em
E F G A | B c d e |]
```

This is E natural minor, with the F♯ from the key signature of G major.

## Harmonic minor

In natural minor there is a whole step between the seventh degree and the tonic. The leading tone is missing: the note a half step below the tonic that pushes the melody to close. **Harmonic minor** brings it back by raising the seventh degree a half step: in A minor, G becomes G♯.

```abc
X:3
L:1/4
K:Am
A B c d | e f ^g a |]
```

This is the form you meet most often. The raised seventh degree doesn't go into the key signature: it is written as an accidental during the piece, every time it is needed. A G♯ that keeps coming back in a piece with no accidentals in the key signature is therefore a strong clue that the piece is in A minor, not in C major. In chapter 7 this clue becomes a method.

Between the sixth and seventh degrees of harmonic minor, in A minor between F and G♯, there is a step and a half, three half steps. It is a distinctive leap, and when singing it, it is easy to go flat on the G♯: think of it as high.

## Melodic minor

**Melodic minor** avoids that leap by also raising the sixth degree on the way up: in A minor, F becomes F♯ and G becomes G♯. On the way down it usually goes back to the natural form, because the leading tone isn't needed when moving away from the tonic.

```abc
X:4
L:1/4
K:Am
A B c d | e ^f ^g a | a =g =f e | d c B A |]
```

It goes up with F♯ and G♯, then comes down with G and F natural.

In the Nota Bene exercises, melodic minor is always the ascending form.

## Three forms, one key

The three forms are not three different keys. A piece in A minor can use G in one place and G♯ in another, depending on where the melody is going. The three forms are a way of describing the notes a piece in minor has at its disposal.
