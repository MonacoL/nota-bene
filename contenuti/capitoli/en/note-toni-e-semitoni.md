Before rhythm, before scales, a singer needs two things: to know which note is written and how far it is from the one next to it. This chapter puts them in order, from the staff to the keyboard.

## The staff

Music is written on the **staff** (UK: stave), five horizontal lines with four spaces between them. Lines and spaces are counted from the bottom: the first line is the lowest, the fourth space the highest. A note sits either on a line, with the line running through its middle, or in a space. The higher it sits, the higher it sounds.

```abc
X:1
L:1/4
K:C
E F G A | B c d e | f4 |]
```

Above, the first note sits on the first line, the second in the first space, and so on, alternating line and space up to the fifth line.

When a note is too low or too high to fit inside the five lines, it gets short dashes drawn just for it, called ledger lines. You read them as extra lines that continue the staff downward or upward.

```abc
X:2
L:1/2
K:C
C A, | a c' |]
```

The first two notes have one and two ledger lines below the staff, the last two have one and two above it.

## The names of the notes

Notes have seven names: C, D, E, F, G, A, B. After B you start again from C, one step higher. The distance from one C to the next C is called an octave, and the same goes for every other note.

Since the names repeat, in Nota Bene every note also carries a number, the number of its octave. **C4** is middle C, roughly in the middle of the piano keyboard. Going up come D4, E4 and so on up to B4; the next note is C5. Going down, the note below C4 is B3. The number always changes at C and nowhere else.

Some keyboards and music programs call middle C "C3". Nota Bene uses the most widespread international numbering, with C4 in the middle, and always sticks to it.

The examples in this chapter are in treble clef, the one sopranos and altos read; the other clefs come in chapter 2. In treble clef, C4 sits on the ledger line just below the staff.

```abc
X:3
L:1/4
K:C
C D E F | G A B c |]
```

This scale goes up from C4 to C5, through every note in between.

## The keyboard as a map

Even if you don't play the piano, the keyboard is the simplest way to see the distances between notes. The white keys are the seven notes in a row, C D E F G A B, and then C again. The black keys come in groups of two and three, alternating.

C is always the white key just to the left of a group of two black keys. Once you have found a C, you find every other note by counting white keys to the right.

## Whole steps and half steps

The **half step** (UK: semitone) is the distance between two neighboring keys, with no key in between, whether they are white or black. It is the smallest distance used in the music we sing. A whole step (UK: tone) is two half steps.

Between C and D there is a black key: from C to the black key is a half step, from the black key to D another one, so C and D are a whole step apart. Between E and F, and between B and C, there is no black key, so those two pairs of white notes are only a half step apart. All the other pairs of neighboring white keys are a whole step apart.

```abc
X:4
L:1/2
K:C
E F | B c |]
```

E4 and F4, then B4 and C5: these are the two half steps between white notes. They are worth learning by heart, because they come back in every chapter. A major scale, for example, is exactly a fixed row of whole steps and half steps.

## Sharp, flat, and natural

The black keys don't have names of their own: they borrow the name of a nearby white key, with a sign in front. The sharp (♯) raises a note by a half step, so F♯4 is the black key just to the right of F4. The flat (♭) lowers it by a half step, and B♭4 is the black key just to the left of B4. The natural (♮) cancels a sharp or a flat and brings the note back to its white key.

These signs are called accidentals, and they are written in front of the note, at the same height. An accidental also applies to the notes that follow it on the same line or in the same space, up to the next bar line, the vertical line that closes the measure (UK: bar). After the bar line it no longer applies.

```abc
X:5
L:1/4
K:C
^F G F =F | B _B A2 |]
```

In the first measure the third note is still F♯4, even without a sign, because the sharp lasts until the bar line; the natural on the fourth note brings it back to F4. In the second measure B4 goes down to B♭4 and then to A4, one half step at a time.

## One note, two names

The black key between F and G can be called F♯ or G♭: it is the same key and the same sound, with two names. Notes like these are called enharmonic. It happens with white keys too: E♯ is the key of F, C♭ the key of B.

Which of the two names is written depends on the key of the piece, which you will meet in chapter 5. For now it is enough to know that whether you sing F♯ or G♭, you produce the same sound.
