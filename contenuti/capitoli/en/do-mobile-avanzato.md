Not every note in a piece belongs to its scale. Some are altered just for a moment, others signal that the music has moved into another key. Movable do has a tool for each of the two cases: chromatic syllables and the do shift.

## Chromatic syllables

When a note is altered outside the key signature, its syllable changes vowel. If the note is raised a half step, the vowel becomes i: DO becomes DI, and likewise RE becomes RI, FA becomes FI, SOL becomes SI, LA becomes LI.

If it is lowered a half step, the vowel becomes e: MI becomes ME, and likewise SOL becomes SE, LA becomes LE, TI becomes TE. The exception is RE, which lowered becomes RA.

The SI of harmonic minor, which you saw in chapter 9, is one of these syllables: it is SOL raised.

```abc
X:1
L:1/4
K:C
C ^C D ^D | E F ^F G |]
w: DO DI RE RI MI FA FI SOL
```

```abc
X:2
L:1/4
K:C
c B _B A | _A G _G F |]
w: DO TI TE LA LE SOL SE FA
```

Going up, altered notes usually have a sharp and take the i; going down they have a flat and take the e. F♯ and G♭ are the same key, but in C major the first is sung FI and the second SE: what decides is the written name, not the sound.

Raised MI and TI, and lowered DO and FA, are missing. Raising MI a half step lands on the key of FA, and the same goes for the other three cases: they are very rare, and they don't appear in Nota Bene.

## Raised or lowered, compared with the key signature

A note is raised or lowered compared with its syllable in the key, not compared with the white key. The written sign alone isn't enough to decide.

In F major, B♭ is in the key signature and is sung FA. A B with a natural is a half step higher: it is a raised FA, and it is sung FI.

```abc
X:3
L:1/4
K:F
F G A =B | c B A G |]
w: DO RE MI FI SOL FA MI RE
```

In the same way, in D major the C♯ from the key signature is TI, and a C with a natural is a lowered TI, that is TE. So when you find a natural sign, ask yourself whether the note goes up or down compared with the key signature.

## The do shift

Sometimes an accidental isn't a passing color: it keeps coming back, the melody leans on a new note, and the music settles there as if it were a new tonic. This is a modulation: the piece has moved into another key. Singing it all with chromatic syllables would be awkward and would lose the sense of the new tonic.

So movable do moves DO onto the new tonic, and from that point on the syllables are those of the new key. This move is called a **do shift**.

```abc
X:4
M:4/4
L:1/4
K:C
C E G c | B A G2 |
w: DO MI SOL DO TI LA DO
D E ^F G | A ^F G2 |]
w: SOL LA TI DO RE TI DO
```

The first notes are in C major. The G in the second measure is where the do shift happens: from there DO moves to G, and the F♯ that follows is no longer a chromatic FI but the TI of the new key, G major. Without the do shift the second half would be sung RE MI FI SOL, LA FI SOL.

The do shift is usually made on a note that belongs to both keys, like the G above. It is SOL in C major and DO in G major, and it is best to think of it with its new name.

## Recognizing a modulation

The clues are the ones you use to find the tonic in chapter 7, applied to a stretch of the piece:

- a new accidental keeps coming back, and it often sits a half step below a note the melody then leans on, like F♯ below G;
- a phrase ends on a note other than the starting tonic;
- if the piece goes back after the modulation, the new accidental disappears and the old one returns.

The most common modulations go to neighboring keys on the circle of fifths, with one more sharp or one fewer flat, or the other way around.

There is one case where the do shift isn't needed: moving between a key and its relative. From C major to A minor DO stays on C, because minor is sung on LA. Just sing SI for G♯.

## Who decides where to shift

Where to place the do shift is a choice, and there isn't always a single answer. An isolated accidental is sung with the chromatic syllable; a new key that lasts a few measures is sung with a do shift. In the cases in between, whoever prepares the reading decides, or the conductor in rehearsal.

In the Nota Bene advanced readings, the solution changes the syllables from the do shift onward. If your conductor places it somewhere else, follow your conductor: what matters is singing the whole passage with the same DO.
