The conductor moves their hand steadily, and the whole choir counts on that movement. This chapter explains what is being counted: the beats, how they are grouped into measures, and how the score says so.

## Measures and beats

The **beat** is the steady pulse music is counted on, the one you naturally tap with your foot. Beats come in groups: the score is divided by vertical lines, the bar lines, and the space between two bar lines is called a measure. In a piece, every measure usually holds the same number of beats.

The first beat of every measure is the strongest. When the conductor beats time, the first beat is the downward gesture.

## The time signature

At the start of the piece, after the clef, there is a kind of fraction: the **time signature**. In simple time the top number says how many beats are in a measure, and the bottom one which note value gets one beat: 4 means quarter note, 2 half note, 8 eighth note.

```abc
X:1
M:2/4
L:1/4
K:C
B B | B2 | B B | B2 |]
```

```abc
X:2
M:3/4
L:1/4
K:C
B B B | B2 B | B3 |]
```

```abc
X:3
M:4/4
L:1/4
K:C
B B B B | B2 B2 | B4 |]
```

In 2/4 you count two quarter notes per measure, in 3/4 three, in 4/4 four.

4/4 is often written as a C instead of numbers. A C with a vertical line through it (₵), called cut time (UK: alla breve), means 2/2 instead: two half-note beats per measure. The notes are the same as in 4/4, but you count them in two, and the music feels more spacious.

## Dividing the beat in two or in three

Every beat can be divided into smaller parts. In simple time it divides in two: a quarter note is worth two eighth notes, and eighth notes come in groups of two.

In **compound time** every beat divides in three: the beat is a dotted quarter note, worth three eighth notes, and eighth notes come in groups of three. In compound time the bottom number is almost always 8, and the top number divided by three gives the beats:

- 6/8 has two dotted-quarter beats;
- 9/8 has three;
- 12/8 has four;
- 3/8 has just one, which is why it feels like a time signature counted "in one".

When a 3/8 is very slow, the conductor may also beat it as three eighth notes. In the Nota Bene exercises you will find it among the compound time signatures, with one beat.

3/4 and 6/8 have measures of the same length, six eighth notes. What changes is how they are grouped, and so how they are counted.

```abc
X:4
M:3/4
L:1/8
K:C
BB BB BB | B2 B2 B2 |]
```

```abc
X:5
M:6/8
L:1/8
K:C
BBB BBB | B3 B3 |]
```

In 3/4 you count "one-and, two-and, three-and": three beats divided in two. In 6/8 you count "one-and-a, two-and-a": two beats divided in three. Many lullabies and barcarolles are in 6/8.

## The pickup

Not every piece starts on the first beat. Often the first measure is incomplete and holds only its last beats: the piece is said to start with a pickup (UK: anacrusis), and the conductor's gesture for those notes is an upbeat.

```abc
X:6
M:3/4
L:1/4
K:C
G | c2 B | A G A | c2 |]
```

Here the piece starts on the third beat. The last measure has only two beats: the pickup and the ending, put together, make one whole measure.

In rehearsal, a pickup entrance means breathing on the beat before, together with the conductor's gesture, and singing on the next one.

## Tempo

The time signature says how the measures are counted, not how fast. The speed is the **tempo**, written above the first staff with a word (Adagio, Andante, Allegro) or with a metronome mark such as ♩ = 72, meaning 72 quarter notes per minute.

Keep the two apart: the time signature is the fraction at the start of the piece, the tempo is how fast you go. If you sing with Italian speakers, beware that in Italian "tempo" usually means the time signature.
