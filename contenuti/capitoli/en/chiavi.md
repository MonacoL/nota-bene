The staff on its own doesn't say which note sits on which line. The clef does: the sign drawn at the start of every staff. In a choral score you will find three of them, and which one you read depends on the voice part you sing.

## What the clef is for

The clef fixes the name of one line. From that line on, notes are counted up and down, one line or space at a time, with the names in order: C D E F G A B.

Change the clef, and the same position on the staff takes another name. That is why, when you open a score, the first thing to look at is the clef on your staff.

## Treble clef

The **treble clef** grew out of a letter G, which is why it is also called the G clef. Its curl wraps around the second line, and that is where G4 sits. Sopranos and altos use it.

```abc
X:1
L:1/2
K:C clef=treble
G C |]
```

The first note is G4, on the second line. The second is C4, on the ledger line below the staff.

To find notes quickly, it helps to remember the lines from the bottom, E4 G4 B4 D5 F5, and the spaces, F4 A4 C5 E5.

## Octave treble clef

Tenors sing about an octave below sopranos. Writing their part in treble clef would take a lot of ledger lines below the staff, and bass clef would keep them always at the top. So they use the **octave treble clef**: a treble clef with a small 8 under it, meaning that everything sounds an octave lower than you would read it in treble clef.

```abc
X:2
L:1/2
K:C clef=treble-8
G c |]
```

Here the second line is G3, and C4 is in the third space. You read the note names as in treble clef; only the octave number changes, one lower.

Some editions give the tenor part a treble clef without the 8. It is sung an octave lower all the same.

Don't confuse it with what instrumentalists call the "tenor clef": that is a different sign, a C clef used by cellos and trombones, and choral scores don't use it.

## Bass clef

The **bass clef** grew out of a letter F, which is why it is also called the F clef. Its two dots sit above and below the fourth line, and that is where F3 sits. Basses use it.

```abc
X:3
L:1/2
K:C clef=bass
F, C |]
```

The first note is F3, on the fourth line. The second is C4, on the ledger line above the staff.

In bass clef the lines from the bottom are G2 B2 D3 F3 A3, and the spaces A2 C3 E3 G3.

## C4 in the three clefs

Middle C is a good landmark for finding your way: in treble clef it sits on the ledger line below the staff, in octave treble clef in the third space, in bass clef on the ledger line above the staff. Three different positions for the same sound.

This explains something that often happens in rehearsal. If sopranos and tenors have the same written note in the same place on the staff, the tenors sing it an octave lower, and that is how it should be.

## When the voice parts share two staves

Many choral scores put the four voice parts on just two staves, like piano music: sopranos and altos on the treble clef staff, tenors and basses on the bass clef one. In these editions tenors read in bass clef, usually with their note stems pointing up, while basses have their stems pointing down.

So it pays to be able to read at least two clefs: the one for your voice part, and the one your part may end up in when the voice parts are gathered together.
