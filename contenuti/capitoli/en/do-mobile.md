In a choir, knowing that a note is a D4 matters less than knowing what role it plays in the key: whether it is the tonic, whether it sits above or below it, whether it is about to return to it. Movable do gives each note a name that says exactly this. With this chapter you have everything you need to start the readings.

## The seven syllables

In **movable do** the tonic of a major key is sung DO, whatever note it is. The other degrees of the scale follow in order: DO RE MI FA SOL LA TI. These words are called syllables, and they are written in capitals so they are not confused with note names.

```abc
X:1
L:1/4
K:D
D E F G | A B c d |]
w: DO RE MI FA SOL LA TI DO
```

In D major, DO is D4. The syllables follow the scale, so F♯4 is MI and C♯5 is TI.

The seventh degree is called TI. Some traditions call it SI, but here the syllable SI is kept for another use, which you will see later in this chapter.

The syllables carry the distances with them. Between MI and FA and between TI and DO there is always a half step, in any key; between all the other neighboring syllables there is a whole step. A leap from DO to SOL is always a perfect fifth, from DO to MI always a major third. Once you learn the sound of DO SOL or MI DO in one key, you find it the same in all the others.

## Note and syllable

A note has a fixed name: D4 is D4 in any piece. Its syllable, on the other hand, depends on the key. In C major D4 is sung RE, in D major DO, in B♭ major MI.

```abc
X:2
L:1/4
K:G
G A B c | d e f g |]
w: DO RE MI FA SOL LA TI DO
```

Here the key is G major, and D5 is SOL. F♯5, from the key signature, is TI.

## Finding DO from the key signature

DO is the tonic of the major key that has that key signature, and you find it with the rules from chapter 5: with sharps it is a half step above the last sharp, with flats it is the second-to-last flat. With no accidentals DO is C, with one flat it is F.

Then, on the staff, it pays to look for where DO sits: on which line or in which space. From there you count the other syllables up and down, one line or space at a time, without going through the note names. It works the same way in all three clefs: only the position of DO on the staff changes.

## Minor is sung on LA

In minor keys the tonic is sung LA. Since the relative minor has the same key signature as the major, the syllables don't change: in A minor, A is sung LA and C is sung DO, exactly as in C major.

```abc
X:3
L:1/4
K:Em
E F G A | B c d e |]
w: LA TI DO RE MI FA SOL LA
```

E minor has one sharp, like G major, and DO is G. The tonic E is sung LA.

So the procedure stays the same for every piece: from the key signature you find DO, then with chapter 7 you work out whether the passage is major or minor. In major the tonic is DO, in minor it is LA.

## SI in harmonic minor

In harmonic minor the seventh degree is raised a half step, as you saw in chapter 6. In A minor, G becomes G♯. The syllable SOL, raised, becomes SI.

```abc
X:4
L:1/4
K:Am
A B c d | e f ^g a |]
w: LA TI DO RE MI FA SI LA
```

SI is the first chromatic syllable you meet, that is a syllable for a note altered outside the key signature. The others come in chapter 10, but this one turns up in almost every piece in minor, and that is why you learn it right away. When you see SI, you know that LA almost always comes right after.

## Before a reading

Faced with a new reading, it pays to always follow the same steps:

1. From the key signature find DO, and look for where it sits on the staff.
2. Decide whether the passage is major or minor, that is whether the tonic is DO or LA.
3. Find the syllable of the first note.
4. Before you start, sing the scale in your head from the tonic up to the first note.

The starting pitch in the readings gives you the tonic chord and the first note, as the conductor does in rehearsal. From there on you sing by syllables, thinking of the distance from the previous one.
