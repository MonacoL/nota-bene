When you read a part, you move from one note to the next with a leap that is bigger or smaller. Each of these moves is an interval. Recognizing intervals on the page and by ear is a large part of what sight-singing takes.

## The number

An **interval** is the distance between two notes, and it is named with a number and a quality. You find the number by counting the note names, including the first and the last. From C to E there are C, D, E: three notes, so it is a third. From C to G there are C, D, E, F, G: a fifth. Two identical notes form a unison, two neighboring Cs an octave.

Accidentals don't change the number. From C to E♭ is still a third, because the note names are still C, D, E.

On the staff you can see the number without even reading the notes. Two notes on neighboring lines, or in neighboring spaces, form a third. From one line to the one after next, skipping one, there is a fifth. Intervals with an odd number go from line to line or from space to space; those with an even number from line to space.

```abc
X:1
L:1/2
K:C
"^5th"C G | "^3rd"C E | "^4th"C F |]
```

## The quality

The same number can correspond to different distances: C and E are four half steps apart, D and F only three, and yet both are thirds. The quality tells you which one it is, and you find it by counting half steps.

Unisons, fourths, fifths, and octaves are called perfect. Seconds, thirds, sixths, and sevenths can be major or minor, and the minor one has one half step less than the major one.

| Interval | Half steps |
|---|---|
| minor and major second | 1 and 2 |
| minor and major third | 3 and 4 |
| perfect fourth | 5 |
| perfect fifth | 7 |
| minor and major sixth | 8 and 9 |
| minor and major seventh | 10 and 11 |
| perfect octave | 12 |

```abc
X:2
L:1/2
K:C
"^major 3rd"C E | "^minor 3rd"D F | "^minor 3rd"C _E |]
```

There is also a shortcut. If the higher note belongs to the major scale of the lower note, the interval is major or perfect. E is in the C major scale, so C and E form a major third; E♭ is not, and C and E♭ form a minor third.

## Fifth, third, and fourth

It pays to learn three intervals first, in this order: the fifth, the third, and the fourth. They are the ones that hold up the melodies of choral pieces and their chords.

The perfect fifth is the distance between the first and fifth degrees of the scale, between tonic and dominant. It sounds full and stable, and it is the leap many pieces use to set off.

The third is the distance between the first and third degrees. Major or minor, it is the third that decides the mode, as you saw in chapter 7. Tonic, third, and fifth together form the chord a key rests on.

The perfect fourth is the distance between the fifth degree and the tonic above it. It is the typical leap of a pickup that lands on the tonic.

The other intervals are learned later, often starting from these: a sixth, for example, is a fifth widened by one degree.

## The tritone

Between the perfect fourth and the perfect fifth there is an interval of six half steps, exactly half an octave: the tritone, so called because it spans three whole steps. In C major it is the distance between F and B.

It is written as an augmented fourth (F and B) or as a diminished fifth (B and F), depending on the note names. It is one of the hardest intervals to sing in tune, because it doesn't lean on any of the nearby sounds.

```abc
X:3
L:1/2
K:C
"^augmented 4th"F B | "^diminished 5th"B f |]
```

## Augmented and diminished

The tritone is the most common case of a general rule. An interval with one half step more than major or perfect is called augmented; one with one half step less than minor or perfect is called diminished. From C to G♯, for example, there is an augmented fifth. In choral music these intervals, apart from the tritone, are rare.

## Melodic and harmonic

An interval is melodic when the two notes are sung one after the other, as in your part. It is harmonic when they sound together, as between your voice and the one next to you. The number and quality are found the same way.

```abc
X:4
L:1/2
K:C
C G | [CG]2 |]
```

The first measure is a melodic fifth, the second the same fifth as a harmonic interval.

## Inversion

If the lower note of an interval moves up an octave, the two notes swap places and the interval changes: this is its inversion. From C4 to E4 there is a major third; from E4 to C5 a minor sixth.

```abc
X:5
L:1/2
K:C
"^major 3rd"C E | "^minor 6th"E c |]
```

Inversion follows fixed rules:

- the numbers of an interval and its inversion always add up to 9: a third becomes a sixth, a second a seventh, a fourth a fifth;
- major becomes minor and minor becomes major;
- perfect stays perfect;
- augmented becomes diminished and diminished becomes augmented.

Inversion helps with recognizing large intervals. A rising minor sixth lands on the same note as a falling major third, an octave higher: if you hear the third, you find the sixth.
