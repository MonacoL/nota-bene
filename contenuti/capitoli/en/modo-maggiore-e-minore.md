A key signature belongs to two keys, a major one and its relative minor. To know which of the two a piece uses, you have to look at the music. This chapter explains how to find the tonic and then the mode, that is whether the passage is major or minor.

## The tonic

The tonic is the note the passage rests on. The melody circles around it, moves away from it and comes back, and when it lands on the tonic it feels like it has come home. That is why a piece almost always ends on the tonic, and very often starts on the tonic or on a note of its chord, the third or fifth degree.

So the first clue is the last note. This holds above all for the basses, who almost always sing the tonic at the end of a piece. The other voice parts, on the other hand, can end on the third or fifth degree: if you sing alto or tenor, your last note is not necessarily the tonic.

## The mode

Once you have found the tonic, the mode depends on the third degree, that is on the distance between the tonic and the note two degrees above it. In major it is four half steps: C and E, G and B. In minor it is three: A and C, E and G. This is the difference that makes a major passage sound more open and a minor passage more inward.

The two melodies below have the same key signature, with no accidentals.

```abc
X:1
M:4/4
L:1/4
K:C
C E G E | F D B, D | E D C2 |]
```

```abc
X:2
M:4/4
L:1/4
K:C
A c e c | d B ^G B | c B A2 |]
```

The first stays around C4, dwells on C, E, and G, and ends on C4: it is in C major. The second stays around A4, dwells on A, C, and E, has a G♯, and ends on A4: it is in A minor.

## How to go about it

Faced with a new passage, it pays to always follow the same steps:

1. Look at the key signature and find the two possible keys, the major one and its relative minor (chapters 5 and 6).
2. Look at the first note and, above all, the last one: which of the two tonics do they point to?
3. Look for an accidental that keeps coming back, a half step below the minor tonic. In A minor it is G♯, in E minor D♯, in D minor C♯. It is the raised seventh degree of harmonic minor, and if it is there, the passage is almost certainly in minor.
4. Look at which notes the melody lingers on and where it ends its phrases.

The first three steps are almost always enough. When the clues don't agree, look at the passage as a whole; below are the most common cases.

## Cases that can fool you

Many Renaissance and Baroque pieces in minor end on a major chord: the basses' last note is the minor tonic, but above it there is a major third, the so-called Picardy third. It doesn't mean the piece is in major; it is a way of ending typical of the period. To decide the mode, look at the rest of the passage, not just the final chord.

An excerpt of a few measures, moreover, doesn't always end on the tonic: sometimes it stops in the middle of a phrase, perhaps on the fifth degree. In that case the accidentals and the notes the melody dwells on count for more.

Finally, a long piece can change key, and one section can be in A minor and another in C major. Chapter 10 explains how to recognize this change and what it means for those who sing with movable do.

## Why it matters

With movable do, the tonic is sung DO if the mode is major, LA if it is minor. Before reading any passage, then, you need to know where the tonic is and what mode it has: it is the first question worth asking yourself when you open a new score.
