The position on the staff tells you which note to sing. The shape of the note tells you how long to hold it. This chapter is about the second thing: note values, rests, and the signs that make a note longer.

## Note values

The **note value** is how the note is drawn, and it tells you how long it lasts. Each note value is worth half of the one before:

| Note value | How it is drawn | Worth in quarter notes |
|---|---|---|
| whole note (UK: semibreve) | hollow head, no stem | 4 |
| half note (UK: minim) | hollow head with a stem | 2 |
| quarter note (UK: crotchet) | filled head with a stem | 1 |
| eighth note (UK: quaver) | filled head, stem, and one flag | ½ |
| sixteenth note (UK: semiquaver) | filled head, stem, and two flags | ¼ |

When several eighth or sixteenth notes come in a row, their flags join into a straight line, the beam, which makes the groups easier to see.

```abc
X:1
M:4/4
L:1/4
K:C
B4 | B2 B2 | B B B B |
B/B/ B/B/ B/B/ B/B/ |
B/4B/4B/4B/4 B/4B/4B/4B/4 B/4B/4B/4B/4 B/4B/4B/4B/4 |]
```

The measures above all last the same: one whole note, then two half notes, four quarter notes, eight eighth notes, and sixteen sixteenth notes.

Note values don't say how many seconds a note lasts, only how long it lasts compared with the others. How fast the piece goes is decided by the tempo, which chapter 4 covers.

## Rests

Silence is written too, with **rests**. Every note value has its rest, which lasts exactly as long as it does, and takes its name: whole rest, half rest, quarter rest, and so on.

```abc
X:2
M:4/4
L:1/4
K:C
z4 | z2 z2 | z z z z | z/z/ z/z/ z/z/ z/z/ |]
```

The whole rest and the half rest look alike: the first hangs below the fourth line, the second sits on top of the third. The whole rest is also used to mean "the whole measure is silent", however long the measure is.

In a choir, rests count as much as notes. A rest that ends means your voice part has to come back in at that exact moment.

## The dot

A dot right after the note head makes the note longer by half its value. A dotted half note is worth a half note plus a quarter note, that is three quarter notes. A dotted quarter note is worth a quarter note plus an eighth note, that is three eighth notes.

```abc
X:3
M:4/4
L:1/4
K:C
B3 B | B3/ B/ B2 |]
```

In the first measure, a dotted half note and a quarter note; in the second, a dotted quarter note, an eighth note, and a half note. The two measures last the same.

Rests can be dotted too, with the same rule.

## Two different curves

Above or below the notes you will often find curved lines. They look alike, but they mean different things.

The **tie** joins two notes of the same pitch. Their lengths add up, and you sing a single held note, without starting it again. It is used above all to hold a note across the bar line, or to write lengths that no single note value can give.

The **slur** joins notes of different pitch. It tells you to sing them smoothly, without breaking between them. In choral pieces it often shows that several notes are sung on the same syllable of the text.

```abc
X:4
M:4/4
L:1/4
K:C
B2- B B | (c d e2) |]
```

In the first measure the tie joins the first two notes: you sing one B4 lasting three quarter notes, then another B4. In the second measure the slur joins three different notes.

A common mistake in choirs is cutting off a long note too early, especially at the end of a phrase. In 4/4 a whole note lasts four full beats, and you hold it to the end of the last one.

## In the exercises

The exercises in this chapter use measures of four quarter notes, the 4/4 time signature. The next chapter explains what measures and time signatures are; for now, just count how many quarter notes are missing to get to four.
